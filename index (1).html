Know your customer and your offer

Lesson 1 of 710 min

Most posts fail because they speak to everyone. When you try to reach everyone, nobody feels the post is meant for them. Start with one type of customer.

Describe one customer

  • Who are they? Think of age, job and place.
  • What problem do they want solved?
  • Which app do they already open every day?

Write your offer in one sentence

Use this pattern: I help [who] get [result] with [what you sell].

Example: I help small cafés get more walk-in customers with eye-catching Instagram posts.

Example: I help office workers in Nairobi get tailored shirts delivered in one week.

Choose where to start

Pick one or two platforms, not five. Go where your customer already spends time.

  • WhatsApp and Facebook: good for local customers who like to chat before they buy.
  • Instagram: good for products that look good in photos, such as food, fashion, beauty and crafts.
  • TikTok: short videos that can reach people who do not follow you yet.
  • LinkedIn: good for services, business customers and professional skills.

Set up profiles that sell

Lesson 2 of 710 min

When someone likes one of your posts, the next thing they do is open your profile. If it is unclear, they leave. A good profile answers three questions in five seconds: what do you sell, who is it for and how do I order?

The five parts of a strong profile

  • Name and photo: use the same name and the same logo or face photo on every platform so people recognise you.
  • Bio: say what you sell, who it is for, where you work and how to order.
  • One link: send people to the place where you want them to go, such as your WhatsApp chat link, your catalog or your website.
  • Contact button: turn on the call, message or email button so ordering takes one tap.
  • Pinned or featured post: show your best offer or best customer result at the top.

Example bio: Fresh lunch boxes for busy workers in Kigali. Order before 10:00, delivered by 12:00. Tap the link to order on WhatsApp.

Use a business account

On Instagram, TikTok and Facebook, switch to a business or professional account. It is free and shows statistics about who sees your posts. You will use those numbers in lesson 6.

Try WhatsApp Business

WhatsApp Business is a free app for small businesses. It lets you add a business profile, a product catalog, quick replies for common questions and labels to organise customers. Features differ between countries, so check WhatsApp's help pages for the current steps.

Decide what to post

Lesson 3 of 712 min

Consistency beats perfection. In a 2026 study of more than 52 million posts, Buffer found that accounts that skipped a week grew followers below their own normal rate, and that posting one or two times a week was clearly better than staying silent. Two or three good posts a week is enough to start, if you can keep it up.

Five kinds of posts

  • Show: your product or service in real use.
  • Teach: one useful tip your customer can use today.
  • Prove: customer photos, reviews and results.
  • Behind the scenes: how you make it and who is on your team.
  • Offer: a clear invitation to buy or to message you.

A common rule of thumb is about four helpful posts for every one direct offer. It is a guideline, not a law, so test what works for your audience.

Pick the right format

  • Carousels (several slides you swipe): in Buffer's data they had the highest engagement on LinkedIn, about 21.8%, and on Instagram, about 6.9%.
  • Short videos: on Instagram, Reels reached more people than carousels, while carousels earned more engagement. Videos shot on a phone can work better than polished ones.
  • Photos and text: still useful, especially on Facebook, where all formats performed close together.

Buffer's data comes from posts scheduled through Buffer, so use the numbers as a guide, not a rule for every country.

A simple post formula

  • Hook: the first line or the first seconds make people stop.
  • Value: give the tip, the proof or the story.
  • Call to action: tell people what to do next, for example "Message me to order."

A weekly rhythm to start with

  • Monday: Teach.
  • Wednesday: Show.
  • Friday: Prove, and an Offer every second week.

Starter ideas

  • The three mistakes customers make when buying what you sell.
  • How your product is made, in 30 seconds.
  • A before and after.
  • The question customers ask you most.
  • A customer story, with their permission.
  • Your price list and how to order.
  • Meet the person behind the business.
  • A myth and the fact about your product.

Treat posting time as a test, not a rule. Try two different times for a month and keep the better one.

Turn attention into sales

Lesson 4 of 712 min

Likes do not pay your bills. Conversations do. This lesson is about what you do after someone shows interest.

Reply to every comment

Buffer found that posts where the account replied to comments had higher engagement than the same account's other posts: about 30% higher on LinkedIn and about 21% higher on Instagram. This shows a link, not proof that replying is the cause. Replying costs nothing, so try it.

  • Reply within a few hours when you can.
  • Ask a question back to keep the conversation going.
  • Give the price in public when you can, and move details such as address and payment to private messages.

A simple sales conversation

  1. Greet the person and thank them.
  2. Ask what they need.
  3. Recommend one option, not ten.
  4. Give the price, the delivery time and how to pay.
  5. Ask for the order: "Shall I prepare it for you today?"
  6. Follow up after delivery and ask for a review.

Three replies you can copy

Change the words in brackets.

Hello, thanks for your message. What are you looking for today?

The price is [price] and delivery takes [time]. Send me your location and I will confirm the total.

Hello, did your order arrive well? If you are happy, could you send me a photo or one sentence about it? I will only post it with your permission.

Collect proof

After each happy sale, ask for a photo or a one-sentence review, and ask permission before you post it. Real customer proof is the strongest post you can make.

Track your leads

A notebook or a simple spreadsheet is enough. Use these columns: name, where they came from, what they want, status (new, talking or paid) and the date to follow up.

Your first paid ad

Lesson 5 of 712 min

Ads can speed things up, but only after the basics work. Run an ad when you have a clear offer, a profile that makes ordering easy and a few posts that people responded to. Then put money behind the post that already did best. Paid ads amplify what works. They do not fix what does not.

Steps in Meta Ads Manager

This covers Facebook and Instagram ads.

  1. Prepare: have a Facebook Page and a payment method added.
  2. Choose an objective. Meta offers six: Awareness, Traffic, Engagement, Leads, App promotion and Sales. Many local businesses start by sending people to a message chat on WhatsApp, Messenger or Instagram.
  3. Set the audience: location, age and language first, then interests. Keep it broad at the start.
  4. Set a budget. The minimum is $1 per day per ad set. Below roughly $5 a day you mostly learn direction, not firm results.
  5. Make the ad: one clear image or short video, short text and always a call-to-action button. Check image sizes so nothing is cropped.
  6. Publish and wait. Review usually takes up to 24 hours. Wait three to seven days before you judge. Day-one results are often misleading.

Menus and names change from time to time. Check Meta's Help Center for the current steps. This course is not affiliated with Meta.

Cost per click varies widely by country and industry. One US-based figure quoted by Buffer puts the average across industries at $1.72, ranging from $0.45 for apparel to $3.77 for finance. Your local cost may be very different. After the first week, use your own Ads Manager numbers.

Budget safety: You do not need to run an ad to complete this course. First test your offer with organic posts and conversations. If you do advertise, use money you can afford to lose, confirm the current platform rules and billing currency, and set a strict spending limit before publishing.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with no goal.
  • Judging the ad after one day.
  • Spending so little that you learn nothing.
  • Changing many things at once. Change one thing at a time, such as the image or the text, so you know what made the difference.

Measure and improve

Lesson 6 of 710 min

You only need a few numbers, and you only need to look at them once a week.

  • Reach: how many people saw your post.
  • Engagement: reactions, comments, shares and saves.
  • Clicks or messages: people who took a step toward buying.
  • Sales: the number that pays you.

Engagement rate

Engagement rate is engagement divided by reach, times 100. Tools define it slightly differently, and some divide by followers. Pick one method and use it every time you compare.

Compare with yourself

Buffer recommends comparing each platform with its own baseline, not with other platforms. For rough context, Buffer's 2025 medians were about 6.1% on LinkedIn, 5.6% on Facebook, 5.5% on Instagram and 4.6% on TikTok. These come from Buffer users, so use them only as background. Your best comparison is your own last month.

The 20-minute weekly review

  1. Open your statistics for the last seven days.
  2. List your three best and three weakest posts.
  3. Ask what the best ones had in common: the topic, the format or the first line.
  4. Make more of that and stop the weakest kind.
  5. Write one change to test next week.
